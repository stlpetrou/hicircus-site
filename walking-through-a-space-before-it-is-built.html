Walking a Space Before It's Built

Immersive walkthroughs let clients feel scale, sightlines and materials long before construction starts.

Virtual Reality · August 2026

A floor plan tells you a room is five metres wide. It doesn't tell you what five metres feels like from the sofa, looking toward a window wall at sunset. That gap between drawn dimensions and felt space is exactly what a walkthrough closes — and it's why VR has become one of the fastest ways to catch a design problem while it's still cheap to fix.

Building for Real-Time

A render built to be beautiful in a single still isn't automatically ready to be walked through. Every surface, texture and light source has to perform in real time, at a stable frame rate, from any angle a visitor chooses — not just the one camera position we'd normally light for. That means rebuilding the scene with real-time engines in mind from the start: baking lighting where we can, optimizing geometry without losing the details that sell the material, and testing the walkthrough on the actual headset it will ship on, not just a desktop preview.

What Clients Actually Test

Once people put the headset on, they stop asking about finishes and start asking practical questions: can I see the kitchen island from the front door, does the corridor feel tight with two people passing, how much daylight actually reaches the back bedroom at 4pm. Those are the questions a plan and a render both struggle to answer honestly.

Letting a client — or their own buyer — stand in a space before it exists turns abstract approval into a confident decision, and it's far cheaper to move a wall in a headset than on site.
